This is the slide I did not want to make. Grammar improved a lot. Pronunciation did not move at all. And I got slower — 92 wpm down to 76. That is not decline, that is me monitoring myself while I speak. Attention is finite: I bought grammar accuracy with speed.

04

The plateau, stated plainly

Pronunciation score: 69 → 73 → 73 → 73 → 73. Mispronunciation rate, normalised per word spoken: 5.2% across the first ten sessions, 6.0% across the last ten. Fifty-one days of daily practice moved the score about four points, then stopped.

Say

The raw error count looks like it exploded — 37 errors in June, 84 in a single August session. But that is only because I recorded a 24-minute session instead of a 3-minute one. Divide by words spoken and the rate is flat. Any progress chart without a denominator is a chart about how much you recorded, not how well you spoke. That was discovery number one, and it changed how I built everything after it.

05

The level estimate that never moved

“B2 (reaching C1)” — returned on 53 of 54 recordings. The single exception was recording #1, which said “B2 (upper-intermediate)”. Fifty-one days, four hours of speech, one unchanging string.

A metric that never changes is not a measurement. It is a label.

Say

This is a lesson about AI feedback in general: it will confidently produce a summary judgment on every single sample, and the confidence tells you nothing about whether the judgment can discriminate. I keep it on the report. I do not track it. The things worth tracking are the ones that can move.

I start a story in the past and slide into the present within one sentence.

Verbatim, from my own recordings

Say

Look at the second-to-last row. “should play” versus “should have played” — that is the whole problem in one line. Mandarin does not mark this; the timeline lives in context, not on the verb. So under any load — a hard idea, a fast sentence — my verbs snap back to the unmarked form.

The AI’s number-one complaint is my most average sound. My actual worst sound is /r/, which it mentioned a third as often.

Keep this caveat on the slide. These rates are attributed: when a word is flagged, every tracked sound inside it is charged for it. Attempt counts are exact; failure counts are an upper bound, and a sound that keeps company with a genuinely bad one inherits blame it may not deserve. The ranking is directional, not final.

Say

This is the single most valuable thing the project produced. “Mandarin speakers struggle with th” is true as a generalisation and false about me. A language model writing a report reaches for the well-known pattern for my first language — because that is what is in its training data — rather than the pattern in my audio. It is not lying. It is pattern-matching on the stereotype of a Chinese speaker instead of measuring the actual Chinese speaker in front of it. You only catch that with a denominator.

Note “should’ve” heard as “shouldn’t”. Those mean opposite things. I did not fail to hear a decoration — I inverted the meaning of the sentence. That is where a listening problem becomes a comprehension problem.

16

The finding: one weakness, two directions

In my mouth

I say “play” for played, “mop” for mopped. Words ending -ed score 70 against 80 for everything else.

In my ears

I hear “we” for we’re, “could” for could’ve, “help” for helped. 54% of my listening errors are exactly these endings.

The whole talk, in one sentence

These are not two problems. English puts grammar in the weak, unstressed parts of the word. Mandarin does not. So I neither produce them nor perceive them.

Say

This is the payoff. I had been treating speaking practice and listening practice as separate activities, with separate scores, in separate panels. They were measuring the same deficit from two sides.

And it reframes the fix completely. Drilling “th” harder was never going to help. What helps is grammatical endings under reduced stress — produced and perceived — which is a completely different exercise. I only found it because both halves sat in one dataset and I went looking for the overlap.

05

What I learned about measuring learning

17

Five rules I had to discover the hard way

  1. A list of mistakes is useless; only a rate means anything. “Seven bad /θ/” means one thing if I said θ nine times and something else entirely if I said it three hundred. Every count in this talk has a denominator — and that single change reversed my top-priority sound.
  2. Consistency is the skill; peak performance is noise. A word counts as mastered when the last N attempts all clear X, not when one lucky take does. Mine is set to last 3 ≥ 85. Across 1,798 drill attempts I average 91 and only 9 fell below 60 — yet far fewer words are actually mastered than that average implies, because once is not a skill.
  3. Position changes the sound. The /l/ in light and the /l/ in feel are different articulations, and only the second has no Mandarin equivalent. Averaging them hides exactly what you are looking for. My own numbers: clear /l/ 7.0%, dark /l/ 4.7% — the opposite of what the textbooks predict for a Mandarin speaker, which is itself worth knowing.
  4. Never mix measurement modes on one chart. Exact per-phoneme scoring and word-level attribution give different numbers for identical speech: θ reads about 4% attributed and about 20% exact. Plot both on one line and you get a dramatic cliff exactly where the instrument changed — and you will read it as progress, or as collapse. It is neither.
  5. Thin data must look thin. A rate off 24 attempts swings wildly; a rate off 1,000 does not. So weeks under 40 attempts are drawn hollow, weeks under 10 are not drawn at all, and the line dots across the gap rather than confidently connecting two points through a week that was never measured.
Say

Rule one is the one that generalises past English. Every self-improvement dashboard I have ever used shows me counts. A count of a thing I did more of is not evidence that I got better at it.

19

What I am doing about it

Stop drilling

/θ/ minimal pairs. Already at baseline. Every hour there is an hour not spent on something 2.3× worse.

Stop measuring

The CEFR level string, and any raw error count without a denominator.

  1. /r/ and /ɜr/. 9.9% and 10.1% failure, and /r/ appears in 1,863 of my words. Highest volume × highest rate = highest return, by a distance.
  2. The -ed morpheme, both directions. Produce it — played, learned, missed, mopped — and perceive it — helped vs help, should’ve vs shouldn’t. Same exercise, both halves. This is the one drill that fixes a grammar problem and a pronunciation problem at once.
  3. The eight high-frequency words I say wrong every day: played (56), phase (32), because (51), just (63), game (62), learned (41), missed (67), difficult (56). Not exotic vocabulary — the words I actually use.

The open question. Prosody has sat at 55–58 for 51 days and is my lowest score of all. I have no diagnosis for it yet — and my speaking rate fell 17% while I was fixing grammar. That is next.

20

Closing

I studied English for twenty years and could not have told you which sound I get wrong most often. It took 51 days of measurement to find out that it was /r/ — and that the thing everybody told me to fix was already fine.

Generic feedback is about a population. You are a sample of one.

Say

Close on the part that transfers. The answer mattered less than the fact that the answer was findable. Anyone can be specific about their own weakness. Almost nobody is, because almost nobody keeps the denominator.

  • Produced — 1,568 distinct content words across four hours of speech.
  • Perceived — derived from 454 graded dictation attempts.
  • Surrounding — 718 words logged from 39 photographs of my actual environment. The coverage report asks the useful question: which things I see every day can I still not name?
  • The ledger only ever grows. Deleting a photo leaves coverage byte-identical, so frequency means “times I have met this word”, not “photos still on disk”.
